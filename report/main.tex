\renewcommand{\abstractname}{ABSTRACT}
\begin{abstract}
  Formula One history is divided between structured data, such as results
  and standings, and narrative text, such as race reports and driver
  biographies. We present Gridly, a search system that brings both together
  by combining the Jolpica F1 database with English Wikipedia. A
  reproducible pipeline collects, cleans and links the two sources into a
  collection of 2058 documents about races, drivers, teams and circuits,
  which we characterize. The collection is indexed in Apache Solr, with a
  different text analysis for narrative text, names and categories, and can
  be searched through a web application. We compare a baseline that
  searches all the text as a single field with a setup that weights the
  sections of each document and filters by document type, on thirteen
  information needs whose results were judged by hand.

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Formula One is the highest class of single-seater motorsport, and its
World Championship has been held every year since the first race in 1950
\cite{f1history}. In more than seven decades, the sport has accumulated
a very large historical record, with over a thousand races and hundreds
of drivers, teams and circuits that have come and gone \cite{jolpica}.
Interest in the sport has also grown in recent years: Formula 1 reports
a global fanbase of 827 million in 2025, 63\% more than in 2018
\cite{f1fans}, bringing many new followers who want to learn about its
past but have no easy way to explore it.

This history is available in two forms, which are rarely found together.
Structured data, such as race results, retirement reasons and
championship standings, is maintained by open projects like Jolpica F1
\cite{jolpica}, the successor to the Ergast API. Free text, such as race
reports, driver biographies and team histories, is found mainly in
collaborative encyclopedias like Wikipedia \cite{wikipedia}. Each form
answers different questions: the results show what happened, while the
text explains why. Finding races decided by a late mechanical failure,
for example, requires matching the retirement reason in the results with
the narrative of the race. Answering this kind of question is the
purpose of an information retrieval system \cite{manning2008}, and it
makes Formula One a good domain for one close to a real-world scenario.

In this project, we present Gridly, a search system that integrates both
types of information. We build a collection in which each race, driver,
team and circuit is one document that joins its structured fields from
Jolpica F1 with its text from Wikipedia, index it in Apache
Solr~\cite{solr}, and evaluate how well two retrieval setups answer
questions that depend on the text, such as the one above.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes the dataset, including its sources,
preparation, quality, structure and characterization.
Section~\ref{sec:needs} presents the search scenarios used to evaluate the
system. Section~\ref{sec:ir} explains how the documents are indexed and
searched and the two setups we compare, and Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}
evaluates them. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes and
outlines future work.

\subsection{Data Origin and Description}
\label{sec:sources}
Two complementary sources were selected, which, when combined, cover the full history of
the sport: the Jolpica F1 database and English Wikipedia.

Table~\ref{tab:sources} characterizes both sources in their raw form. For Jolpica, rows and columns are summed over its 11 tables; for Wikipedia, each row corresponds to
an article, and each column to a field stored for it.

    \midrule

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Initial data sources characterization~\cite{jolpica,wikipedia}}
  \label{tab:sources}

Jolpica F1~\cite{jolpica} is an open-source project that succeeds the Ergast
API, which was discontinued at the end of 2024. Its relational database holds the
results of every World Championship race since the inaugural 1950 season, together with additional information such as starting
grids, finishing positions, retirement reasons, team line-ups, and
championship standings. We used its CSV dump instead of the REST API, which
would require around 260 paginated requests under an hourly rate limit.
Wikipedia~\cite{wikipedia} provides the text: the narrative of each race,
the biography of each driver and the history of each team and circuit. The
articles were obtained in wikitext through the MediaWiki Action
API~\cite{mediawikiapi}, returning several articles per request.

The two sources are linked directly through Jolpica, which stores the Wikipedia
URL of each race, driver, team and circuit. This avoids matching by name,
which would be error-prone for drivers with the same name or races whose
title changed over the years. Both sources are released under open licenses (CC
BY-NC-SA 4.0 for Jolpica and CC BY-SA 4.0 for Wikipedia) that allows their use in an academic project.

\subsection{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:pipeline}
The pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) transforms the two raw sources into
a validated collection of documents. It is implemented in Python as five
scripts, each reading the output of the previous one. Raw downloads,
intermediate files and the final collection are kept in separate folders.

\textit{Data Ingestion} downloads the Jolpica dump, keeps the 11 tables needed
and records the version used (12 September 2026), since the dump link
always points to the latest one. \textit{Entity Structuring} then joins these tables
with Pandas~\cite{pandas} into one record per race, driver, team and
circuit, with values such as the winner and retirements of a race or the
career totals of a driver. Jolpica has one team row per chassis--engine
combination (``Cooper-Climax'', ``Cooper-Maserati''), so rows that point to
the same article were merged into one team, and only races already held
were kept.

Each record carries the link to its Wikipedia article, which the
\textit{Wikipedia Crawler} uses to download the text through the MediaWiki API. Since Wikipedia blocks anonymous
clients that send one request per page (HTTP 429), articles are requested
in batches of 20, reducing about 2230 requests to 115. Each article is
cached locally with its page and revision identifiers, which identify the
exact version used and allow later runs to download only what is missing.

The \textit{Wikitext Parser} converts the wikitext to clean text with mwparserfromhell~\cite{mwparserfromhell}, removing tables,
references, infoboxes and images and keeping the visible text of links.
In \textit{Schema Mapping \& Filtering}, each article is split by
its sections, which are mapped by keywords to a fixed set of text fields
per document type (Table~\ref{tab:fields}), and sections without prose,
such as classification tables, are discarded. Documents with
fewer than 100 words of text are rejected, with the reason recorded. The
\textit{Relational Linker} links the accepted ones to each other in both directions (races, drivers
and teams, and races and circuits), keeping only links between documents
that exist in the collection, and exports them in JSON and CSV. Finally,
\textit{Collection Characterization} computes the statistics and plots of
Section~\ref{sec:characterization}, using Matplotlib~\cite{matplotlib},
NumPy~\cite{numpy} and scikit-learn~\cite{scikitlearn}.

The whole pipeline runs with a single \texttt{make} command, which creates
the virtual environment, installs the dependencies and runs the five steps
in order. Each step also has its own target (\texttt{collect},
\texttt{structure}, \texttt{wikipedia}, \texttt{documents} and
\texttt{analyze}), while \texttt{make clean} removes the generated files
but keeps the downloads, and \texttt{make clean-all} removes everything.

\subsection{Data Quality}
\label{sec:quality}
Table~\ref{tab:quality} lists the problems found and how they were handled.
Most come from the links between the sources. In 28 cases, the Jolpica link
leads to a \textbf{disambiguation page} (``Tony Brooks may refer
to\ldots''), usually because a more famous person has the same name.
So that the fix survives a new run of the pipeline, they are resolved
automatically by choosing the entry that mentions ``Formula One'' or
``racing driver'' (e.g., ``Tony Brooks (racing driver)''), and each
correction is recorded with the original link. The Long Beach circuit links
to the article about the city, which is a valid article and cannot be
detected automatically, so it was corrected by hand.

In the results, the \textbf{status} is a numeric code with no
documentation. By grouping the values of the \texttt{detail} column
(``Engine'', ``Spun off'', ``+1 Lap'') under each code, we inferred its
meaning: finished, finished with laps behind, retired by accident, retired
by failure, disqualified and did not start. This made it possible to keep
the retirement reasons, one of the most useful structured fields.

Most of the 164 \textbf{short documents} are drivers from the 1950s with one
or two races, whose article is a single sentence followed by a table. The
threshold was chosen by inspecting these articles: raising it to 150 words would reject 111 more documents, many of them with useful text.

\subsection{Conceptual Model}
\label{sec:model}
The conceptual model (Figure~\ref{fig:model}) describes the documents
produced by the pipeline. An abstract \textbf{Document} holds the fields
shared by all types (identifier, title, word count, Wikipedia
identifiers and the \texttt{summary}). It is specialized into four classes,
\textbf{RaceDocument}, \textbf{DriverDocument}, \textbf{TeamDocument} and
\textbf{CircuitDocument}, each with its own structured attributes and text
sections. Identifiers are canonical and readable, such as
\texttt{race\_2021\_22} or \texttt{driver\_hamilton}, and only documents with at least 100 words are accepted into the collection.

Each race has several drivers and at least two teams, and each driver
participates in one or more races and drives for one or more teams. A team has
at least one driver, not necessarily two: 47 Jolpica teams had a single driver
in their whole history, mostly private entries of the 1950s. A team may have no
linked race, which happens for three teams whose races were all rejected.
Each race is held at exactly one circuit, which hosts one or more races; in the
documents this link is optional, because the Pedralbes circuit was rejected
and its two races have no circuit document. All relations are stored as lists
of identifiers in both directions (e.g., \texttt{driver\_ids} in a race and
\texttt{race\_ids} in a driver), allowing the search system to move from a
document to the related ones.


\subsection{Data Characterization}
\label{sec:characterization}
Document length is very uneven (Figure~\ref{fig:wordcount}). In every type
the mean is well above the median, pulled up by a few long articles: the
longest driver document (Kimi Räikkönen) has 14454 words, twenty times the
median. For drivers, length grows with the career: the number of race
entries and the number of words have a Spearman correlation of 0.69
(Figure~\ref{fig:driverwords}). For races, length depends on the period
(Figure~\ref{fig:decade}): the median goes from about 330 words in the 1960s
to 1849 in the 2000s, almost six times more. The 2000s are the peak, which
suggests that these seasons were written about as they happened by a large
community of editors, while older races were documented later and with
fewer sources. This matters for the search system: a long document has more
chances of matching a query, so a ranking that does not normalize length
well will tend to favor recent races.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{imgs/race_words_by_decade.png}
  \caption{Median words per race document, by decade}
  \Description{Bar chart of median words per race from the 1950s to the 2020s. The values stay between about 320 and 430 until the 1970s, rise to about 650 to 700 in the 1980s and 1990s, peak at about 1850 in the 2000s and then decrease to about 1540 and 1290.}
  \label{fig:decade}
\end{figure}

The collection has 2.79 million tokens and a vocabulary of 34348 terms,
37\% of which appear only once. Stopwords make up 45\% of all
tokens. Term frequency follows Zipf's law~\cite{manning2008} between ranks
10 and about 2000 (Figure~\ref{fig:zipf}), with the usual deviations at both
ends: the most frequent words are less frequent than the law predicts, and
the tail falls faster. The vocabulary grows sublinearly, as Heaps' law
predicts (Figure~\ref{fig:heaps}). As documents are read grouped by type,
the growth almost stops during the races, which reuse the names and terms
of the biographies, and rises again with the teams.

After removing stopwords, ``championship'', ``prix'', ``race'' and
``formula'' appear in about 95\% of the documents, so they will have little
weight in a ranking based on inverse document frequency. The terms specific
to each type are more telling (Figure~\ref{fig:topterms}): races use
``lap'', ``pit'', ``qualifying'' and ``tyres'', teams ``engine'', and
circuits ``track'' and ``turn''.

To see how the language of the race reports changed over time, we
extracted the keywords of each decade with TF-IDF~\cite{manning2008}, using
scikit-learn and removing the names of drivers, teams and circuits, which
would otherwise dominate. Table~\ref{tab:keywords} shows that the keywords
follow the history of the sport: privateer and works entries in the 1950s,
turbo engines in the 1980s, refuelling stops and Friday sessions in the
2000s, and tyre compounds, penalties and the safety car in the 2010s. Some
noise remains, such as ``utc'', from the start times given in recent
articles.

In the structured fields, engine failures
are the most common retirement reason (1889 cases), but accidents,
collisions and spins together exceed 2500 (Figure~\ref{fig:retirements}).
British (142) and American (131) drivers are the most common
(Figure~\ref{fig:nationalities}), the latter because the Indianapolis 500
counted for the championship until 1960. Finally, every race has
a summary and 85\% have a race report, but only 42\% describe qualifying
and 27\% the aftermath (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}), so some queries can only
be answered for part of the collection, mostly recent seasons.

\section{SEARCH SCENARIOS}
\label{sec:needs}
Table~\ref{tab:needs} lists the information needs used to
evaluate the system. They were chosen so that they depend on the text and
not only on the structured fields, cover all four document types, can be
expressed with varied vocabulary, and have answers in the collection, which
we confirmed by finding relevant documents for each. For each need we
defined what makes a document relevant and what does not. In N2, for
example, a race is relevant only if it was affected by rain \textbf{and}
had an unusual result, such as the 1996 Monaco Grand Prix, where only three
cars finished. A wet race won by the favorite is not relevant.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lp{5.4cm}l}
    \toprule
    ID & Information need & Type \\
    \midrule
    N1 & Races decided by a pit-stop or tyre strategy & race \\
    N2 & Wet races with a surprising result & race \\
    N3 & Safety car periods that changed the result & race \\
    N4 & Collisions between teammates & race \\
    N5 & Race weekends with a fatal accident & race \\
    N6 & Disqualifications for technical infringements & race \\
    N7 & Drivers who returned after a serious injury & driver \\
    N8 & Drivers who succeeded at Le Mans or Indianapolis & driver \\
    N9 & Teams that collapsed for financial reasons & team \\
    N10 & Technical innovations that were later banned & team \\
    N11 & Teams that were renamed or taken over by another company & team \\
    N12 & Circuits whose layout was changed after a serious accident & circuit \\
    N13 & Circuits that drivers boycotted or considered too dangerous & circuit \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Information needs}
  \label{tab:needs}
\end{table}

We deliberately included some needs that are hard to satisfy. In N1, the same idea appears as
``undercut'', ``two-stop strategy'' or ``gambled on not needing a pit
stop''; in N4, the text often gives only the two names, so the
\texttt{teams} field is needed; N7 has few documents that use the words of
the query, even though there are well-known cases such as Niki Lauda or
Felipe Massa; and in N10, ``banned'' mostly returns the ban on turbo
engines, which affected every team and is not relevant. These cases expose
the limits of a search based only on words.

\section{INFORMATION RETRIEVAL}
\label{sec:ir}
To search the collection we use Apache Solr~\cite{solr}, an open-source
search platform. Solr receives the documents, organizes their content in an
index that makes search fast, and answers each query with a list of documents
ordered by relevance. This order is computed with BM25~\cite{manning2008},
Solr's default ranking function. BM25 gives more weight to words that are rare
in the collection and compensates for document length, which matters here
because the length of our documents is very uneven
(Section~\ref{sec:characterization}). Solr runs in a Docker container, and a
single script creates the container, defines the fields and loads the
documents, so the whole index can be rebuilt at any time.

\subsection{What Is Indexed}
\label{sec:indexing}
Each race, driver, team and circuit is a separate document, so every search
result is one complete entity that a user can read on its own. Relations
between documents are kept as lists of identifiers, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:model}.

Not all fields should be searched in the same way. A race report is free text,
a driver's name must not be altered, and a season is a number. We therefore
grouped the fields into the five kinds shown in Table~\ref{tab:schema}. All
fields are indexed except the Wikipedia URL, which is only displayed.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{1.6cm}p{3.3cm}p{2.3cm}}
    \toprule
    Kind & Examples & Search behavior \\
    \midrule
    Narrative text & summary, race report, biography, team history & full text
    analysis \\
    Names & title, winner, drivers, teams, circuit & ignores case and accents \\
    Categories & document type, country, nationality & exact value \\
    Numbers & season, wins, starters & exact value or range \\
    Dates & race date, date of birth & exact date or range \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Kinds of indexed fields}
  \label{tab:schema}
\end{table}

\subsection{Text Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
Before being indexed, text goes through an analyzer: a tokenizer splits it
into words, and a sequence of filters then transforms those words. Narrative
text goes through the following steps:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tokenization} splits the text at spaces and punctuation.
  \item \textbf{Possessive removal} turns ``Senna's'' into ``Senna''.
  \item \textbf{Accent folding} adds a version without accents, so that
  ``Raikkonen'' also finds ``Räikkönen''.
  \item \textbf{Lowercasing} makes searches independent of case.
  \item \textbf{Stop word removal} discards very common English words such as
  ``the'' or ``in'', which carry little meaning and make up 45\% of all the
  tokens in the collection.
  \item \textbf{Stemming} reduces words to a common root with the Porter
  algorithm, so that ``crashed'' and ``crashing'' match each other.
\end{itemize}

For example, the sentence \textit{``Räikkönen's crashing in the rain''} is
reduced to the terms \textit{raikkonen}, \textit{räikkönen}, \textit{crash}
and \textit{rain}. The same steps are applied to the queries, so that both
sides end up in the same form. Names only go through tokenization, accent
folding and lowercasing, because removing word endings could change a name
and stop words can be part of one.

We did not add a list of synonyms. Words that describe the same idea, such as
``undercut'' and ``two-stop strategy'', are included by hand in the query of
each need, and handling them automatically is left for the final system.

\subsection{Retrieval Setups}
\label{sec:setups}
We compare two setups. Both use eDisMax, the Solr query parser designed for
free text, and both run the same query for each information need. A query
lists the main words of the need and their common variants: for N4 it contains
\textit{teammates}, \textit{team-mate}, \textit{collided}, \textit{collision},
\textit{crashed} and \textit{together}. A document does not need to contain all
of them to be returned, and documents are always ordered by relevance alone.
Table~\ref{tab:setups} describes the parameters that were used.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.6cm}p{3cm}p{1.3cm}p{1.85cm}}
    \toprule
    Name & Meaning & Baseline & Boosted \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{q} & Words to search for & same & same \\
    \texttt{qf} & Fields to search and their weights & one field & several
    (Table~\ref{tab:weights}) \\
    \texttt{pf2} & Bonus when pairs of query words appear close together & not
    used & several (Table~\ref{tab:weights}) \\
    \texttt{ps2} & Maximum distance between words for that bonus & not used & 3
    positions \\
    \texttt{fq} & Restricts the results without changing their score & not
    used & type of the need \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Query parameters of each setup}
  \label{tab:setups}
\end{table}

The \textbf{baseline} represents a minimal configuration. The title and all
Wikipedia sections are copied into a single field, so a word counts the same
wherever it appears, and the search covers the whole collection.

The \textbf{boosted} setup uses what we know about the structure of the
documents (Table~\ref{tab:weights}). The summary has the highest weight, since
it concentrates the main facts of an article, and the sections that narrate
what happened count more than background information. Documents receive a
bonus when consecutive words of the query appear close together, such as
\textit{crashed} and \textit{together} in N4. We apply this bonus to pairs of
words because our queries are lists of keywords: a bonus that required all of
them to appear together had no effect on any need. Results are restricted to
the document type requested by each need. The lists of teams and
drivers are also searched because a race text often names two drivers without

\section{EVALUATION}
\label{sec:evaluation}
We evaluate both setups on the thirteen information needs of
Table~\ref{tab:needs}. For each need, we wrote down beforehand what makes a
document relevant and what does not, so that every judgement follows the
same rule. Appendix~\ref{sec:criteria} lists these criteria together with
the query and filter of each need, and Section~\ref{sec:detailed} describes
the four hardest needs in more detail. For example, a race is relevant to N1 only
if the text says that the strategy decided who won; a race that only mentions
routine pit stops, or where the strategy changed positions outside the fight
for the victory, is not.

\textbf{Judgements.} Each setup returns its top 10 documents for every need.
The two lists are merged without duplicates, which gives between 15 and 20
documents per need, and each document is judged by hand as relevant (1) or
not relevant (0). To decide, we read an excerpt of the document in which the
query words are highlighted and, when that is not enough, the full article.
The relevant documents of a need are those judged relevant in this merged
list, so recall is measured relative to it and not to the whole collection.

\textbf{Metrics.} For each need and setup we compute precision at 10 (P@10),
the share of relevant documents in the first page of results, and average
precision (AP), which also rewards placing the relevant documents near the
top. The mean of the AP values over all needs (MAP) gives a single number per
setup. We also plot the interpolated precision at eleven recall levels,
averaged over all needs (Figure~\ref{fig:pr}).

\subsection{Detailed Information Needs}
\label{sec:detailed}
The four needs below are described in more detail because they are the
hardest to satisfy with a search based on words
(Section~\ref{sec:needs}); the criteria of the other needs are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:criteria}. For each one we give the full list of
parameters sent to Solr by the boosted setup. Three of them are the same for
every need:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{qf}: \texttt{title\^{}2 summary\^{}3 background
  qualifying\^{}2 race\^{}2 post\_race\^{}2 other biography\^{}2
  history\^{}2 layout events teams drivers}
  \item \texttt{pf2}: \texttt{summary\^{}4 qualifying\^{}2 race\^{}3
  post\_race\^{}2 biography\^{}3 history\^{}3}
  \item \texttt{ps2}: \texttt{3}
\end{itemize}
The baseline sends the same \texttt{q}, \texttt{q.op}, \texttt{fl},
\texttt{rows} and \texttt{sort}, but with \texttt{qf=all\_text}, without
\texttt{pf2} and \texttt{ps2}, and without \texttt{fq}, so it searches the
whole collection.

\paragraph{N1: Races decided by a pit-stop or tyre strategy.}
\textit{Relevance judgement.} The goal is to find Formula One races whose winner was decided by the
strategy of the teams: the number of pit stops, the lap on which a driver
stopped, the choice of tyres, an undercut or overcut, or a gamble on
stopping fewer times than the rivals. Since almost every modern race report
describes pit stops, a race is not relevant when the stops were routine, or
when the strategy only changed positions outside the fight for the victory.
The 2019 Hungarian Grand Prix, won by Hamilton after switching to a two-stop
strategy to pass Verstappen in the last laps, is relevant.

\textit{Search.} The query combines the general terms (\textit{pit stop},
\textit{strategy}, \textit{tyres}) with the specific forms in which the idea
is written (\textit{undercut}, \textit{two-stop}, \textit{one-stop},
\textit{gamble}), since no single word covers every case. Results are
restricted to races and ordered by score, so races in which several of these
words appear in the summary or close together in the race report come
first.

\textit{Query.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{defType}: \texttt{edismax}
  \item \texttt{q}: \texttt{"pit stop" strategy undercut tyres "two-stop" "one-stop" gamble}
  \item \texttt{q.op}: \texttt{OR}
  \item \texttt{qf}, \texttt{pf2}, \texttt{ps2}: as above
  \item \texttt{fq}: \texttt{type:race}
  \item \texttt{fl}: \texttt{id, title, score}
  \item \texttt{rows}: \texttt{10}
  \item \texttt{sort}: \texttt{score desc}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{N7: Drivers who returned after a serious injury.}
\textit{Relevance judgement.} The goal is to find Formula One drivers whose biography describes a
serious injury that interrupted their career for some time \textbf{and}
their later return to racing, as with Niki Lauda after his burns in 1976.
Injuries that did not stop the career, injuries that ended it and fatal
accidents are not relevant, even though their biographies use many of the
same words.

\textit{Search.} The query covers both halves of the need: the injury
(\textit{serious}, \textit{injury}, \textit{injured}, \textit{hospital},
\textit{burns}) and the return (\textit{recovered}, \textit{returned},
\textit{comeback}). Results are restricted to drivers, and biographies where
the two halves appear close together receive the word-pair bonus. The query
cannot tell an injury followed by a return from one followed by retirement
or death, which is why this need is hard.

\textit{Query.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{defType}: \texttt{edismax}
  \item \texttt{q}: \texttt{serious injury injured recovered returned comeback hospital burns}
  \item \texttt{q.op}: \texttt{OR}
  \item \texttt{qf}, \texttt{pf2}, \texttt{ps2}: as above
  \item \texttt{fq}: \texttt{type:driver}
  \item \texttt{fl}: \texttt{id, title, score}
  \item \texttt{rows}: \texttt{10}
  \item \texttt{sort}: \texttt{score desc}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{N10: Technical innovations that were later banned.}
\textit{Relevance judgement.} The goal is to find Formula One teams that created or pioneered a
specific technical solution that the regulations later banned, such as a fan
car or a six-wheeled car. A ban that applied to every team in the same way
without being aimed at an innovation of that team, such as the end of turbo
engines in 1989, is not relevant, and neither is an innovation that was
never banned.

\textit{Search.} The query joins the idea of a new solution (\textit{innovation}) with the
words for a ban (\textit{banned}, \textit{outlawed}, \textit{prohibited}),
and adds the names of the best-known banned solutions (\textit{fan car},
\textit{six-wheeled}, \textit{ground effect}) as phrases. Results are
restricted to teams. The word \textit{banned} also appears in the history of
many teams because of the turbo ban, so documents that only mention that
general ban can still rank high.

\textit{Query.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{defType}: \texttt{edismax}
  \item \texttt{q}: \texttt{innovation banned outlawed prohibited "fan car" "six-wheeled" "ground effect"}
  \item \texttt{q.op}: \texttt{OR}
  \item \texttt{qf}, \texttt{pf2}, \texttt{ps2}: as above
  \item \texttt{fq}: \texttt{type:team}
  \item \texttt{fl}: \texttt{id, title, score}
  \item \texttt{rows}: \texttt{10}
  \item \texttt{sort}: \texttt{score desc}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{N13: Circuits that drivers boycotted or considered too dangerous.}
\textit{Relevance judgement.} The goal is to find Formula One circuits that drivers or teams boycotted
or refused to race at for safety reasons, or that were described as too
dangerous. Boycotts for political or contractual reasons are not relevant,
and neither are circuits where accidents happened without any criticism of
the safety of the track itself.

\textit{Search.} The query includes the actions of the drivers (\textit{boycott},
\textit{boycotted}, \textit{refused}, \textit{protest}, \textit{criticized})
and the judgement of the track (\textit{dangerous}, \textit{unsafe},
\textit{safety concerns}). Results are restricted to circuits. Words such as
\textit{dangerous} and \textit{drivers} also appear in plain descriptions of
accidents, and \textit{boycott} in political disputes, so some circuits match
without being relevant.

\textit{Query.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{defType}: \texttt{edismax}
  \item \texttt{q}: \texttt{boycott boycotted refused dangerous unsafe "safety concerns" protest criticized drivers}
  \item \texttt{q.op}: \texttt{OR}
  \item \texttt{qf}, \texttt{pf2}, \texttt{ps2}: as above
  \item \texttt{fq}: \texttt{type:circuit}
  \item \texttt{fl}: \texttt{id, title, score}
  \item \texttt{rows}: \texttt{10}
  \item \texttt{sort}: \texttt{score desc}
\end{itemize}

\section{CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We built a reproducible collection of 2058 Formula One documents,
characterized it and defined thirteen information needs for our future work.

The most challenging part was turning Wikipedia into usable text: the API
blocks one-request-per-page clients, the wikitext mixes prose with tables
and templates, and some links led to disambiguation pages. These were
solved with batched and cached requests, a parser that keeps only the
prose, and automatic link resolution.

For the final system, we plan to add semantic search with text embeddings,
combined with BM25 in a hybrid ranking, and compare it with the current
setups on the harder information needs, where the same idea is written in
different ways. We will also explore synonyms and query expansion for racing
vocabulary, and extend the web application with result snippets and filters
over the structured fields, such as season and nationality.
