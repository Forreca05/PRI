\renewcommand{\abstractname}{ABSTRACT}
\begin{abstract}
  Formula One history is divided between structured data, such as results and standings, and narrative text, such as race reports and driver biographies. We present Gridly, a search system that combines Jolpica F1 with English Wikipedia. A reproducible pipeline collects, cleans and links both sources into a document collection, which we characterize and use to define information needs for evaluating future search approaches.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Formula One is the highest class of single-seater motorsport, and its
World Championship has been held every year since the first race in 1950
\cite{f1history}. In more than seven decades, the sport has accumulated
a very large historical record, with over a thousand races and hundreds
of drivers, teams and circuits that have come and gone \cite{jolpica}.
Interest in the sport has also grown in recent years: Formula 1 reports
a global fanbase of 827 million in 2025, 63\% more than in 2018
\cite{f1fans}, bringing many new followers who want to learn about its
past but have no easy way to explore it.

This history is available in two forms, which are rarely found together.
Structured data, such as race results, retirement reasons and
championship standings, is maintained by open projects like Jolpica F1
\cite{jolpica}, the successor to the Ergast API. Free text, such as race
reports, driver biographies and team histories, is found mainly in
collaborative encyclopedias like Wikipedia \cite{wikipedia}. Each form
answers different questions: the results show what happened, while the
text explains why. Finding races decided by a late mechanical failure,
for example, requires matching the retirement reason in the results with
the narrative of the race. Answering this kind of question is the
purpose of an information retrieval system \cite{manning2008}, and it
makes Formula One a good domain for one close to a real-world scenario.

In this project, we present Gridly, a search system that integrates both
types of information. The remainder of this paper is organized as
follows: Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes the dataset, including its sources,
preparation, quality, structure, and characterization. Section~\ref{sec:needs}
presents the search scenarios, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes and outlines
future work.

\subsection{Data Origin and Description}
\label{sec:sources}
Two complementary sources were selected, which, when combined, cover the full history of
the sport: the Jolpica F1 database and English Wikipedia.

Table~\ref{tab:sources} characterizes both sources in their raw form. For Jolpica, rows and columns are summed over its 11 tables; for Wikipedia, each row corresponds to
an article, and each column to a field stored for it.

    \midrule
    Jolpica F1 & CSV & 11 & 141029 & 110 & 7.30 \\
    Wikipedia & JSON & 2225 & 2225 & 7 & 77.37 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Initial data sources characterization~\cite{jolpica,wikipedia}}
  \label{tab:sources}
\end{table}

Jolpica F1~\cite{jolpica} is an open-source project that succeeds the Ergast
API, which was discontinued at the end of 2024. Its relational database holds the
results of every World Championship race since the inaugural 1950 season, together with additional information such as starting
grids, finishing positions, retirement reasons, team line-ups, and
championship standings. We used its CSV dump instead of the REST API, which
would require around 260 paginated requests under an hourly rate limit.
Wikipedia~\cite{wikipedia} provides the text: the narrative of each race,
the biography of each driver and the history of each team and circuit. The
articles were obtained in wikitext through the MediaWiki Action
API~\cite{mediawikiapi}, returning several articles per request.

The two sources are linked directly through Jolpica, which stores the Wikipedia
URL of each race, driver, team and circuit. This avoids matching by name,
which would be error-prone for drivers with the same name or races whose
title changed over the years. Both sources are released under open licenses (CC
BY-NC-SA 4.0 for Jolpica and CC BY-SA 4.0 for Wikipedia) that allows their use in an academic project.

\subsection{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:pipeline}
The pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) transforms the two raw sources into
a validated collection of documents. It is implemented in Python as five
scripts, each reading the output of the previous one. Raw downloads,
intermediate files and the final collection are kept in separate folders.

\textit{Data Ingestion} downloads the Jolpica dump, keeps the 11 tables needed
and records the version used (12 September 2026), since the dump link
always points to the latest one. \textit{Entity Structuring} then joins these tables
with Pandas~\cite{pandas} into one record per race, driver, team and
circuit, with values such as the winner and retirements of a race or the
career totals of a driver. Jolpica has one team row per chassis--engine
combination (``Cooper-Climax'', ``Cooper-Maserati''), so rows that point to
the same article were merged into one team, and only races already held
were kept.

Each record carries the link to its Wikipedia article, which the
\textit{Wikipedia Crawler} uses to download the text through the MediaWiki API. Since Wikipedia blocks anonymous
clients that send one request per page (HTTP 429), articles are requested
in batches of 20, reducing about 2230 requests to 115. Each article is
cached locally with its page and revision identifiers, which identify the
exact version used and allow later runs to download only what is missing.

The \textit{Wikitext Parser} converts the wikitext to clean text with mwparserfromhell~\cite{mwparserfromhell}, removing tables,
references, infoboxes and images and keeping the visible text of links.
In \textit{Schema Mapping \& Filtering}, each article is split by
its sections, which are mapped by keywords to a fixed set of text fields
per document type (Table~\ref{tab:fields}), and sections without prose,
such as classification tables, are discarded. Documents with
fewer than 100 words of text are rejected, with the reason recorded. The
\textit{Relational Linker} links the accepted ones to each other in both directions (races, drivers
and teams, and races and circuits), keeping only links between documents
that exist in the collection, and exports them in JSON and CSV. Finally,
\textit{Collection Characterization} computes the statistics and plots of
Section~\ref{sec:characterization}, using Matplotlib~\cite{matplotlib},
NumPy~\cite{numpy} and scikit-learn~\cite{scikitlearn}.

The whole pipeline runs with a single \texttt{make} command, which creates
the virtual environment, installs the dependencies and runs the five steps
in order. Each step also has its own target (\texttt{collect},
\texttt{structure}, \texttt{wikipedia}, \texttt{documents} and
\texttt{analyze}), while \texttt{make clean} removes the generated files
but keeps the downloads, and \texttt{make clean-all} removes everything.

\subsection{Data Quality}
\label{sec:quality}
Table~\ref{tab:quality} lists the problems found and how they were handled.
Most come from the links between the sources. In 28 cases, the Jolpica link
leads to a \textbf{disambiguation page} (``Tony Brooks may refer
to\ldots''), usually because a more famous person has the same name.
So that the fix survives a new run of the pipeline, they are resolved
automatically by choosing the entry that mentions ``Formula One'' or
``racing driver'' (e.g., ``Tony Brooks (racing driver)''), and each
correction is recorded with the original link. The Long Beach circuit links
to the article about the city, which is a valid article and cannot be
detected automatically, so it was corrected by hand.

In the results, the \textbf{status} is a numeric code with no
documentation. By grouping the values of the \texttt{detail} column
(``Engine'', ``Spun off'', ``+1 Lap'') under each code, we inferred its
meaning: finished, finished with laps behind, retired by accident, retired
by failure, disqualified and did not start. This made it possible to keep
the retirement reasons, one of the most useful structured fields.

Most of the 164 \textbf{short documents} are drivers from the 1950s with one
or two races, whose article is a single sentence followed by a table. The
threshold was chosen by inspecting these articles: raising it to 150 words would reject 111 more documents, many of them with useful text.

\subsection{Conceptual Model}
\label{sec:model}
The conceptual model (Figure~\ref{fig:model}) describes the documents
produced by the pipeline. An abstract \textbf{Document} holds the fields
shared by all types (identifier, title, word count, Wikipedia
identifiers and the \texttt{summary}). It is specialized into four classes,
\textbf{RaceDocument}, \textbf{DriverDocument}, \textbf{TeamDocument} and
\textbf{CircuitDocument}, each with its own structured attributes and text
sections. Identifiers are canonical and readable, such as
\texttt{race\_2021\_22} or \texttt{driver\_hamilton}, and only documents with at least 100 words are accepted into the collection.

Each race has several drivers and at least two teams, and each driver
participates in one or more races and drives for one or more teams. A team has
at least one driver, not necessarily two: 47 Jolpica teams had a single driver
in their whole history, mostly private entries of the 1950s. A team may have no
linked race, which happens for three teams whose races were all rejected.
Each race is held at exactly one circuit, which hosts one or more races; in the
documents this link is optional, because the Pedralbes circuit was rejected
and its two races have no circuit document. All relations are stored as lists
of identifiers in both directions (e.g., \texttt{driver\_ids} in a race and
\texttt{race\_ids} in a driver), allowing the search system to move from a
document to the related ones.


\subsection{Data Characterization}
\label{sec:characterization}
Document length is very uneven (Figure~\ref{fig:wordcount}). In every type
the mean is well above the median, pulled up by a few long articles: the
longest driver document (Kimi Räikkönen) has 14454 words, twenty times the
median. For drivers, length grows with the career: the number of race
entries and the number of words have a Spearman correlation of 0.69
(Figure~\ref{fig:driverwords}). For races, length depends on the period
(Figure~\ref{fig:decade}): the median goes from about 330 words in the 1960s
to 1849 in the 2000s, almost six times more. The 2000s are the peak, which
suggests that these seasons were written about as they happened by a large
community of editors, while older races were documented later and with
fewer sources. This matters for the search system: a long document has more
chances of matching a query, so a ranking that does not normalize length
well will tend to favor recent races.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{imgs/race_words_by_decade.png}
  \caption{Median words per race document, by decade}
  \Description{Bar chart of median words per race from the 1950s to the 2020s. The values stay between about 320 and 430 until the 1970s, rise to about 650 to 700 in the 1980s and 1990s, peak at about 1850 in the 2000s and then decrease to about 1540 and 1290.}
  \label{fig:decade}
\end{figure}

The collection has 2.79 million tokens and a vocabulary of 34348 terms,
37\% of which appear only once. Stopwords make up 45\% of all
tokens. Term frequency follows Zipf's law~\cite{manning2008} between ranks
10 and about 2000 (Figure~\ref{fig:zipf}), with the usual deviations at both
ends: the most frequent words are less frequent than the law predicts, and
the tail falls faster. The vocabulary grows sublinearly, as Heaps' law
predicts (Figure~\ref{fig:heaps}). As documents are read grouped by type,
the growth almost stops during the races, which reuse the names and terms
of the biographies, and rises again with the teams.

After removing stopwords, ``championship'', ``prix'', ``race'' and
``formula'' appear in about 95\% of the documents, so they will have little
weight in a ranking based on inverse document frequency. The terms specific
to each type are more telling (Figure~\ref{fig:topterms}): races use
``lap'', ``pit'', ``qualifying'' and ``tyres'', teams ``engine'', and
circuits ``track'' and ``turn''.

To see how the language of the race reports changed over time, we
extracted the keywords of each decade with TF-IDF~\cite{manning2008}, using
scikit-learn and removing the names of drivers, teams and circuits, which
would otherwise dominate. Table~\ref{tab:keywords} shows that the keywords
follow the history of the sport: privateer and works entries in the 1950s,
turbo engines in the 1980s, refuelling stops and Friday sessions in the
2000s, and tyre compounds, penalties and the safety car in the 2010s. Some
noise remains, such as ``utc'', from the start times given in recent
articles.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lp{6.3cm}}
    \toprule
    Decade & Keywords \\
    \midrule
    1950s & manuel, british, entered, privateer, argentine \\
    1960s & manufacturers, cup, british, works, entered \\
    1970s & cup, manufacturers, austrian, south, swedish \\
    1980s & tag, turbo, brazilian, frenchman, italian \\
    1990s & pre, report, failed, spun, failure \\
    2000s & friday, constructors, stops, sessions, saturday \\
    2010s & turn, penalty, tyre, compound, safety \\

  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Top TF-IDF keywords of race documents by decade}
  \label{tab:keywords}
\end{table}

In the structured fields, engine failures
are the most common retirement reason (1889 cases), but accidents,
collisions and spins together exceed 2500 (Figure~\ref{fig:retirements}).
British (142) and American (131) drivers are the most common
(Figure~\ref{fig:nationalities}), the latter because the Indianapolis 500
counted for the championship until 1960. Finally, every race has
a summary and 85\% have a race report, but only 42\% describe qualifying
and 27\% the aftermath (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}), so some queries can only
be answered for part of the collection, mostly recent seasons.

\section{SEARCH SCENARIOS}
\label{sec:needs}
Table~\ref{tab:needs} lists the information needs used to
evaluate the system. They were chosen so that they depend on the text and
not only on the structured fields, cover all four document types, can be
expressed with varied vocabulary, and have answers in the collection, which
we confirmed by finding relevant documents for each. For each need we
defined what makes a document relevant and what does not. In N2, for
example, a race is relevant only if it was affected by rain \textbf{and}
had an unusual result, such as the 1996 Monaco Grand Prix, where only three
cars finished. A wet race won by the favorite is not relevant.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lp{5.4cm}l}
    \toprule
    ID & Information need & Type \\
    \midrule
    N1 & Races decided by a pit-stop or tyre strategy & race \\
    N2 & Wet races with a surprising result & race \\
    N3 & Safety car periods that changed the result & race \\
    N4 & Collisions between teammates & race \\
    N5 & Race weekends with a fatal accident & race \\
    N6 & Disqualifications for technical infringements & race \\
    N7 & Drivers who returned after a serious injury & driver \\
    N8 & Drivers who succeeded at Le Mans or Indianapolis & driver \\
    N9 & Teams that collapsed for financial reasons & team \\
    N10 & Technical innovations that were later banned & team \\
    N11 & Teams that were renamed or taken over by another company & team \\
    N12 & Circuits whose layout was changed after a serious accident & circuit \\
    N13 & Circuits that drivers boycotted or considered too dangerous & circuit \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Information needs}
  \label{tab:needs}
\end{table}

We deliberately included some needs that are hard to satisfy. In N1, the same idea appears as
``undercut'', ``two-stop strategy'' or ``gambled on not needing a pit
stop''; in N4, the text often gives only the two names, so the
\texttt{teams} field is needed; N7 has few documents that use the words of
the query, even though there are well-known cases such as Niki Lauda or
Felipe Massa; and in N10, ``banned'' mostly returns the ban on turbo
engines, which affected every team and is not relevant. These cases expose
the limits of a search based only on words.

\section{INFORMATION RETRIEVAL}
\label{sec:ir}
The collection is indexed in Apache Solr~\cite{solr} 10, running in a Docker
container. A single script creates the container, loads the schema and
indexes the documents, so the index can be rebuilt from scratch at any time.
Solr ranks documents with BM25~\cite{manning2008}, its default similarity.

\subsection{Document Model and Indexing}
\label{sec:indexing}
Each race, driver, team and circuit is one flat Solr document, identified by
\texttt{id} and labeled by \texttt{type}. Entities are linked by identifier
fields instead of nested documents, so each result is always one complete
entity. Table~\ref{tab:schema} summarizes how the fields are indexed.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{1.3cm}p{3cm}p{2.85cm}}
    \toprule
    Type & Processing & Fields \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{f1\_text} & Standard tokenizer, English possessives, ASCII
    folding, lowercase, English stop words, Porter stemming &
    Wikipedia sections (\texttt{summary}, \texttt{race},
    \texttt{biography}, \texttt{history}, \ldots) and \texttt{all\_text} \\
    \texttt{f1\_name} & Standard tokenizer, ASCII folding, lowercase &
    \texttt{title}, \texttt{winner}, \texttt{drivers}, \texttt{teams},
    \texttt{circuit}, \ldots \\
    \texttt{string} & None (exact value) & \texttt{type}, \texttt{country},
    \texttt{nationality}, identifiers \\
    Numeric & None & \texttt{season}, \texttt{wins}, \texttt{starters},
    \ldots \\
    Date range & None & \texttt{date}, \texttt{date\_of\_birth} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Indexed field types}
  \label{tab:schema}
\end{table}

Narrative text is stemmed so that different word forms match, while names
are not, because stemming would distort them. ASCII folding keeps both the
original and the folded token, so a query without diacritics still finds the
name. The same analysis is applied at index and query time. For example,
\textit{``Räikkönen's crashing in the rain''} produces the tokens
\texttt{raikkonen}, \texttt{räikkönen}, \texttt{crash} and \texttt{rain}.
The \texttt{all\_text} field is a copy of the title and all Wikipedia
sections, used only by the baseline. Date range fields accept the dates in
the format found in the data, such as \texttt{1950-05-13}, and still support
range filters.

\subsection{Retrieval Setups}
\label{sec:setups}
Both setups use the eDisMax query parser and the same query text for each
information need. They return the top 10 documents ordered only by relevance
score, which is never replaced by another sort order. Table~\ref{tab:setups}
shows how they differ.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{1.4cm}p{1.6cm}p{4.1cm}}
    \toprule
    & Baseline & Boosted \\
    \midrule
    Search fields (\texttt{qf}) & \texttt{all\_text} &
    weight 3: \texttt{summary}; weight 2: \texttt{title},
    \texttt{qualifying}, \texttt{race}, \texttt{post\_race},
    \texttt{biography}, \texttt{history}; weight 1: \texttt{background},
    \texttt{other}, \texttt{layout}, \texttt{events}, \texttt{teams},
    \texttt{drivers} \\
    Phrase boost (\texttt{pf}, \texttt{ps}) & None &
    weight 4: \texttt{summary}; weight 3: \texttt{race},
    \texttt{biography}, \texttt{history}; weight 2: \texttt{qualifying},
    \texttt{post\_race}; slop 3 \\
    Filter (\texttt{fq}) & None & \texttt{type} of the need \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Retrieval setups}
  \label{tab:setups}
\end{table}

The boosted setup gives the highest weight to \texttt{summary}, which
concentrates the main facts of an article, and more weight to the sections
that narrate what happened than to background material. The \texttt{teams}
and \texttt{drivers} fields are included because a race text often names two
drivers without saying that they are teammates, as in N4. Weights were set
manually while exploring the queries in the Solr interface.

\section{CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We built a reproducible collection of 2058 Formula One documents,
characterized it and defined thirteen information needs for our future work.

The most challenging part was turning Wikipedia into usable text: the API
blocks one-request-per-page clients, the wikitext mixes prose with tables
and templates, and some links led to disambiguation pages. These were
solved with batched and cached requests, a parser that keeps only the
prose, and automatic link resolution.

As future work, the collection will be indexed in Apache Solr~\cite{solr},
comparing a single-field baseline with a setup that weights the most
informative fields and filters by structured fields, both evaluated with
precision at $k$ and mean average precision. The harder information needs
will then serve to compare this word-based search with semantic search.
